In conversations we've had with CSR managers, roughly the same obstacles keep coming up. This article presents five of them, drawing on what the people concerned tell us, rather than on theory.

The goal isn't to point out a shortcoming, but to help put words on situations that are lived through — and sometimes hard to put into words for management. For each mistake, we suggest a realistic way to fix it, one that doesn't require a large consulting budget or a complex tool.

## Mistake #1 — The master spreadsheet nobody keeps updated

This is the most universally present element. A `trame_RSE.xlsx` file (or an equivalent Google Sheet), often built with care, meant to centralize KPIs, projects and objectives.

After three months, the finding is usually the same: two or three areas are up to date, the others fall behind, no one knows anymore which is the "real version," and some people keep their own local copies. The file ends up existing without being genuinely used.

**Why does this happen?** A single file centralizes the responsibility for updating it on one person. That person has to go and gather the information from everyone else, which becomes impossible as soon as the scope goes beyond two or three topics.

**How to fix it.** Move away from the single file and switch to a shared tool, where each team updates its own data without having to ask permission. This can be a dedicated tool (like Yumni) or, as a starting point, a redesign of the file's structure with one tab per owner and automatic consolidation.

## Mistake #2 — Vague objectives that can't be measured

"Reduce our carbon footprint," "improve diversity," "strengthen our CSR approach." These phrasings appear in nearly all the strategic documents we see. They share one common flaw: six months later, it's impossible to say whether they're actually progressing.

**Why does this happen?** Formalizing SMART objectives (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) requires a precision that's often postponed for lack of time. And as long as no one challenges the wording, it stays as is.

**How to fix it.** Take each vague objective and run it through the SMART filter: *Numeric target + Deadline + Measurement method + Owner*. An objective that can't answer these four questions is still just a slogan. Three real objectives are worth more than fifteen empty phrasings.

## Mistake #3 — Annual reporting that only serves reporting

Once a year, sometimes twice, everything that was done gets gathered in a rush, dressed up as a report, and published. It's a deliverable. But no one then uses it to adjust the following year's strategy. Reporting becomes an administrative obligation disconnected from day-to-day operations.

**Why does this happen?** As long as the report is built after the fact from scattered data, preparing it is so costly that it absorbs all the available energy. There's no time left to analyze it and put it to use.

**How to fix it.** Reverse the logic: generate the report from data that's already entered to manage day-to-day operations. No double entry, no rushed annual reconstruction. This is what modern CSRD tools are meant for (and one of Yumni's promises). For those who don't yet use a dedicated tool: structure your files from the start so the report can come out of them mechanically.

## Mistake #4 — An initiative carried by a single person

The CSR Manager is often alone in their office. Frontline teams don't feel fully involved, for lack of shared tools and KPIs that are visible to them. The result: actions carried out on the ground (energy savings, training, supplier initiatives) never formally make their way up into central management.

**Why does this happen?** Historically, CSR has been treated as a specialist subject, and therefore handed to one expert. But operational management can't be the job of a single person.

**How to fix it.** Take CSR out of the CSR Manager's office and treat it as a cross-functional business topic. Concretely: assign actions and KPIs to the relevant teams (production, HR, purchasing), in a tool they actually use, with shared visibility over their own contributions.

## Mistake #5 — Everything is a priority, so nothing is

Without an explicit prioritization method, every CSR initiative ends up getting the same level of attention — which is to say, not much. The most visible initiatives (often the most "communication-friendly") consume the available resources; the structural but less spectacular initiatives (data collection, process redesign) get postponed.

**Why does this happen?** Prioritizing means naming what you're not going to do. It's a politically costly exercise that many organizations avoid.

**How to fix it.** Adopt a simple method, even an imperfect one. **WSJF** (Weighted Shortest Job First, *Value × Urgency ÷ Effort*), borrowed from agile, works well for CSR: each initiative gets three scores, and the ratio gives an order. The exact method matters less than the fact of prioritizing explicitly and owning that choice.

## Summary

These five mistakes aren't faults: they're the consequence of tools and methods that haven't kept up with the rising demands of CSR in SMEs. Excel was an excellent tool to get started with. It quickly hits its limits as soon as you need to manage five focus areas, ten teams, forty KPIs.

The point isn't to spend tens of thousands of euros on tools, but to **move from a "master file" mode to a "shared management tool" mode**. That's precisely what we're trying to offer with Yumni, at a level of simplicity and pricing suited to different types of organizations.
